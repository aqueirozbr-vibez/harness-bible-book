# Chapter 00 — The heuristic becomes the algorithm

*Part 0 · Word budget 3,000–4,000 · No lab · Verified as of September 2026*

In 2009 Roger Martin drew a funnel. At its wide mouth he put *mystery* — a domain in which people act but cannot say why what works works; the refinery's most experienced planner knows which pump will fail next and could not write the rule. Narrower, he put *heuristic* — a rule of thumb that captures most of the mystery in words an apprentice can follow: check the seal history before the bearing history on any pump older than ten years. At the funnel's neck he put *algorithm* — a procedure so fully specified that it runs without judgment, on a machine, at a cost that falls every year. Martin's argument was about business design: value moves down the funnel, and the firms that win are those that push knowledge from mystery to heuristic to algorithm faster than their rivals, then use the freed attention to attack the next mystery. He wrote it before anyone had a language model that could read a maintenance procedure. It is the most useful frame this book knows for what those models have done, and the frame is why the book is called what it is.

The claim of the frame, applied here, is simple to state and takes forty chapters to make good on. Large language models have collapsed the middle of the funnel. Knowledge that lived as heuristic — in the heads of planners, in the margins of procedures, in the way a good supervisor reads a work request — can now be captured as text a machine consumes, and the machine's behaviour is close enough to the heuristic's that the funnel's neck has moved: what was once a rule of thumb a person applied can now be a step a system performs. But the collapse is partial and treacherous. The model executes the heuristic *probabilistically* — ninety-five times in a hundred, with the failures uncorrelated to anything a person would notice — and an algorithm that runs at ninety-five percent is not an algorithm; it is a heuristic wearing an algorithm's clothes. The work of turning the model's behaviour into something a plant can run is the work of the harness, and the harness is the subject of this book. The heuristic becomes the algorithm not when the model can perform it, but when the surrounding system can guarantee that it did.

A second frame arrived in 2023 and describes the same shift from the user's side. Jakob Nielsen, who had spent forty years measuring how people use computers, announced that the language models had produced the first new user-interface paradigm in sixty years. The first, batch processing, had the user submit a complete job and wait; the second, command-based interaction — every interface since the terminal, including the graphical desktop and the phone — had the user issue a command and the computer execute it, one command at a time, the user specifying the *how*. The third, which he named intent-based outcome specification, has the user state the *what*: the outcome wanted, in words, with the system deciding how. Nielsen was careful about what this meant for design. The user no longer needs to know the commands, which removes the largest barrier to computing that has ever existed; but the user also loses the direct control that made command interfaces predictable, and gets in exchange a system whose behaviour must be trusted rather than known. He predicted that the first generation of intent-based interfaces would be poor — a chat box is a bad way to specify most outcomes — and that the work of the next decade would be building the structures around intent that make it reliable. Three years on, that prediction reads as a description of harness engineering.

Put the two frames together and the shape of the change is visible. From the organization's side, heuristics that were locked in people are becoming executable — the funnel is moving. From the user's side, the way work is asked for is changing from commands to intents — the interface is moving. The two motions meet at the point where a technician types "P-101 is leaking at the seal" and a system produces a work order that a supervisor approves and a planner schedules. That sentence is an intent. What turns it into an outcome is a chain of heuristics the plant used to hold in people: which procedure applies, which permit, which parts, which zone rules, what the last three repairs found. And what makes the outcome trustworthy is neither the model's fluency nor the technician's phrasing, but the structure between them. This book is about that structure. Its three impacts, which the rest of this chapter walks through and the rest of the book engineers, are on the interface, on curation, and on the division of labour.

The first impact is on the interface, and it is the one most organizations meet first and understand least. The intent-based paradigm is not the chat box. The chat box is the demonstration that the paradigm exists — the moment in which a person discovers that stating what they want, in their own words, produces something — and it is also the worst possible production interface, for the reasons Nielsen gave: it is unstructured, so the intent it captures is ambiguous; it is conversational, so the system's behaviour drifts with the conversation; and it is open, so the same box that accepts a fault report accepts a request to skip the permit. A production intent interface is a form that looks like a conversation: it has fields the intent must fill (the asset, the symptom, the requester's identity, the scope), it asks one clarifying question when a field is missing rather than guessing, it confirms what it understood before it acts, and it refuses what its scope does not allow — not by arguing but by stating. The chapter on intake gives this its full form, and the point here is only that the interface change is real and that its production version looks nothing like the demonstration that announced it. The organizations that mistake the demonstration for the product build chat bots and measure engagement; the ones that understand the paradigm build intake and measure resolved tasks.

The interface change has a second half that Nielsen did not emphasize and industrial practice makes unavoidable: the outcome has an interface too. Intent goes in as words; the outcome comes out as a record in a system the plant already runs — a work order in the CMMS, a reservation in the warehouse, a message to a supervisor. The outcome's interface is the receiving system's schema, its permissions, its audit log, and its human approver. A harness that gets the intent right and delivers the outcome as a paragraph has done half the job; the other half is delivery as a validated, gated, recorded write to a system of record, and it is the half that decides whether the plant adopts the thing. The reader who has run an enterprise integration will recognize the shape: the model is new, and the discipline around it — schemas, idempotency, audit, approval — is the discipline that has always been required of anything that writes to a system people depend on.

The second impact is on curation, and it follows from the funnel. When a heuristic lived in a planner's head, the plant's documents did not have to be right in the way an algorithm needs them to be right. The planner read the procedure and corrected for what she knew: that the revision on the server was two behind the one the vendor sent, that the torque table had a typo everyone knew about, that the hazard class had been raised in a review the document did not yet reflect. The model reads what it is given and corrects for nothing. A procedure with a stale revision, a register with a duplicate tag, a history full of abbreviations only one shift understands — each of these was a mystery the heuristic absorbed, and each becomes a failure the algorithm reproduces at scale. The consequence is that the first real work of deploying a language model in a plant is not the model and not the prompt; it is document control. Which documents have an owner. Which have a revision that is actually maintained. Which the plant would stand behind if a machine acted on them. The chapters on data and on retrieval give the mechanics — provenance, revisions, currency filters, a governance precondition the index enforces by refusing what it cannot vouch for — but the frame is the funnel's: an algorithm needs curated inputs in a way a heuristic never did, and the curation is the plant's to do, because no vendor can do it.

Curation has a subtler form that the funnel also predicts. The model's outputs are fluent and plausible, and the organization will be tempted to treat them as knowledge — to store the summaries, to remember the facts the model produced, to let last month's answers inform this month's. That is the mystery re-entering the funnel from the bottom: unvouched heuristics dressed as algorithmic output. The book's treatment of memory is largely a treatment of this temptation, and its rule is the funnel's rule read backwards: nothing the model produced becomes durable knowledge until a person has pushed it through the neck — reviewed it, attached its provenance, and accepted responsibility for it. Records may accumulate; knowledge is curated; and the difference between the two is the difference between a plant that improves and one that slowly fills with confident errors.

The third impact is on the division of labour, and it is the one the executive reader should read most carefully, because it is where the funnel's promise and its cost meet. Martin's point about the funnel was that pushing knowledge to algorithm frees the people who held the heuristic — frees them, in the good case, to work on the next mystery, and displaces them, in the bad case, to nothing. The language models have made that choice concrete and immediate for a class of work — reading, drafting, checking, summarizing, routing — that was never before automatable and that a great deal of skilled industrial labour consists of. What the chapters that follow show, in arithmetic rather than assertion, is that the division does not fall where the demonstrations suggest. The model does not replace the planner. The model performs the planner's routine heuristics at a reliability the harness must raise to acceptable, and the planner's work moves to three places the model cannot go: to specifying what acceptable means — the criteria, the rubrics, the golden cases that turn an intent into a testable outcome; to judging what the harness's sensors cannot check — the two criteria out of eleven that need a person, the gate, the calibration of the machine judges; and to the mystery — the failures the harness clusters and the planner explains, which become next month's rules and next year's sensors. The division of labour that emerges is not people versus machines but people specifying, machines executing, and people verifying, with the boundary between the second and third moving as the harness matures.

There is a fourth thing the funnel predicts that is worth saying plainly, because it is the reason the book has a part on economics and a chapter on procurement rather than ending at the architecture. Algorithms are cheap and heuristics are expensive, and the reason firms push knowledge down the funnel is that the cost per outcome falls. The language models have made a class of heuristic cheap to *execute* — a few cents for a work order that took a planner twenty minutes — while leaving the cost of making it *reliable* where it always was: in the people who specify, verify, and improve. A plant that reads the vendor's price list and multiplies by its task volume has priced the execution and ignored the reliability, and the chapters on economics show, with the plant's own numbers, that the second is the larger line and the one that falls as the harness matures. The heuristic becomes the algorithm at a cost, and the cost is engineering, not tokens.

The frame also explains something the field's vocabulary obscures: why the harness, and not the model, is the object worth engineering. Every vendor's benchmark measures the model, and the model's scores rise on a schedule the vendor sets. But the same model, moved from a simple scaffold to an engineered one, moves its score by as much as a generation of model improvement — the evidence for this is the first thing Part II examines — and the scaffold is the plant's to build, on the plant's schedule, measured on the plant's work. In the funnel's terms, the vendor supplies a component that performs heuristics; the plant supplies the structure that makes them algorithms; and the second is where the plant's advantage over its rivals can live, because every rival can rent the same model. A plant that waits for the next model to make its use case reliable is waiting for a number the vendor does not sell, and the arithmetic of Part I — a ninety-five percent step rate that completes an eleven-step task fifty-seven times in a hundred — is the reason the wait is long.

A word on what the book calls the harness, because the term is new enough to need placing. A harness is the set of components around a model that turn its outputs into outcomes a plant can rely on: the intake that structures intent, the guides the model reads, the context assembled for each step, the retrieval that brings the plant's knowledge, the control plane that makes the work durable, the loop that verifies each step, the tools that act, the sensors that check, the permissions that bound, the gate where a person decides, the records that make it all auditable, and the loop by which the plant improves it. The word was chosen over *framework* and *platform* because those describe software one buys; a harness is something one fits, to a particular animal, for particular work, and adjusts as the animal and the work change. The book's position is that the harness is a discipline — harness engineering — with its own measurements, failure modes, and economics, and that a plant which learns it will be able to use any model, from any vendor, on any task its knowledge can specify.

The book that follows is organized around this frame in a way the reader should know before starting. Part I is about the model — what it is, how it reads and writes, where its behaviour comes from, why it fails the way it does, and what it costs. Its purpose is not to make the reader a model engineer but to give the reader the specific, quantitative understanding of the model's failure modes that harness design depends on: a reader who knows why an equipment tag fragments in the tokenizer, why two pumps embed near each other, why the model's stated confidence is not its accuracy, and why a long context is worse than a short retrieved one, will make different — and better — decisions about intake, retrieval, sensors, and gates. Part II is the harness: twelve components, each a chapter, each with a mechanism, a reference implementation, a measurement, a failure table, and a lab that reproduces its numbers. It is the funnel's neck, built. Part III is the plant: which use cases to choose, how to sit the system beside operational technology without ever touching it, what the regulations ask, what it costs, how to buy it, who runs it, and where it goes. A reader with an executive brief to write can read Part III alone and be well served; a reader who must build should read Part II with the labs open; a reader who wants to know why the harness is shaped as it is should start with Part I.

Two commitments run through every chapter and the reader is entitled to hold the book to them. The first is that every number is either derived by code the reader can run, cited to a source the reader can check with its tier stated, or labelled as illustrative; there are no round numbers offered as facts. The second is that every chapter names what it does not know — the measurement that has not been published, the figure that is a vendor's on the vendor's example, the claim that the plant's own golden set must replace — because a book that pretends to certainty about a field this young would be doing to its reader what an uncalibrated model does to a planner. The harness is the discipline of not trusting the fluent answer; the book tries to practise it.

One scenario recurs from the first chapter to the last, so that the arithmetic has somewhere to live: a refinery maintenance agent, two thousand queries a day, a seal leak on pump P-101 in a restricted zone, eleven steps from a technician's message to an approved work order, and a rule the book states in every part — the agent never touches a control system. The scenario is invented and the plant is not named, but the numbers in it are built from public prices, published benchmarks, and the labs' own runs, and a reader in a plant will find the shape familiar. The point of the scenario is not that every reader runs a refinery; it is that the funnel's arithmetic only means something when it is carried through one case with real values, and that a book about turning heuristics into algorithms should show its work.

Martin ended his funnel with a warning that this book adopts as its last word here. The firms that push knowledge to algorithm and then stop — that treat the algorithm as finished and disband the people who understood the heuristic — lose the ability to see the next mystery, and are overtaken by rivals who kept the funnel moving. The harness is not a project with an end date. It is the mechanism by which a plant keeps pushing what it knows through the neck, measures whether the push worked, and keeps the people who can explain the failures close enough to turn them into the next rule. The heuristic becomes the algorithm, and then the algorithm's failures become the next heuristic, and the plant that runs that loop is the one that benefits from the models rather than being surprised by them.
